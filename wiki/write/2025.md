### From: [[../../raw/note/caiFerroelectricitydrivenStrainmediatedMagnetoelectric2025|caiFerroelectricitydrivenStrainmediatedMagnetoelectric2025]]
- This study introduces a novel ultra-low-power approach leveraging vertically integrated two-dimensional van der Waals multiferroic heterostructures composed of Fe3GaTe2 and P(VDF-TrFE). Exploiting strainmediated magnetoelectric coupling, we achieve effective modulation of the magnetic anisotropy constant. Specifically, the inverse piezoelectric effect within the ferroelectric polymer P(VDF-TrFE) induces strain, which, through lattice symmetry breaking, facilitates non-volatile reconfiguration of the magnetic order in Fe3GaTe2 at room temperature. This strainmediated mechanism modulates the magnetic anisotropy constant K1, enabling fully electrically reversible modulation of the anomalous Hall resistance and demonstrating inverter functionality upon application of a polarization voltage. Quantitative validation employing Raman spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, and first-principles calculations establishes a clear correlation between strain, exchange coupling energy, and magnetic anisotropy. Device integration further demonstrates the feasibility of constructing reconfigurable logic gate arrays (AND/NOR/NOT gates) and half-adder circuits. Significantly, these spin logic devices exhibit exceptional performance characteristics, including ultra-low energy consumption (0.5 aJ), high-speed writing (5 ns), and robust operational stability. This research not only advances the fundamental understanding of van der Waals magnetoelectric coupling mechanisms but also provides a scalable platform for next-generation non-volatile memory and reconfigurable logic technologies.

- In previous studies, DFT calculations were performed for magnetic MXenes (Ti-, V-, Cr-, and Mn-based MXenes) and a crystal field framework was proposed to explain the magnetic order of MXene, which was then successful employed to seek robust ferromagnetic materials with high Curie temperature [23,23]. However, the transport properties of some MXenes contradict the models, especially in the Janus MXenes. For example, the Mn 3d at both sides of Mn2NOF is regarded to be partially filled, suggesting the half-metal, but it shows the semiconductor in the DFT calculation. The main deviation arises from the inaccurate consideration of the charge transfer and electron configuration. In previous analysis, the same magnitude of electrons is simply regarded to be donated from two sides, Mn to N, leading to the Mn3+ for all Mn [25]. Thus, any remaining electrons are forced into the eg, leading to half-metal in their model. Similar contradictions could be observed in V2NOF. If the same electrons are regarded to be donated from two sides, V to C (2 electrons), only one V3+ oxidation state should be obtained at both F and O sides, respectively. Therefore, the t2g should be partially filled due to the 3d2 spin state of V3+ and then predicted to be half-metal. However, the V2NOF is a semiconductor from DFT calculation [41]. According to our mechanism, the V at F and O sides should donate one and two electrons to N, leading to the 3d3 and 3d1 spin states of V2+ (F side) and V4+ (O side). Only the V eII and eI at O are empty, while the V dz2 + eII at the F side is fully filled by spin majority electrons but leaves eI empty, leading to the semiconductor V2NOF in DFT calculations.

### From: [[../../raw/note/duUltrasensitiveOptoelectronicBiosensor2025|duUltrasensitiveOptoelectronicBiosensor2025]]
- Recent advances in twistronics have revealed tunable optoelectronic properties in twisted bilayer graphene (tBLG), including angle-dependent dielectric responses and enhanced light absorption due to van Hove singularity (VHS). However, achieving high photoresponsivity in tBLG-based sensors typically requires intense illumination. We present an ultrasensitive optoelectronic biosensor integrating tBLG superlattices with Au nanodisks and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR)-Cas12a via DNA origami. By aligning the 9.4° tBLG's VHS absorption spectrum with Au nanodisks' plasmonic resonance at 60 μW, we achieve a 7-fold photocurrent enhancement over pristine tBLG. CRISPR-Cas12a-mediated trans-cleavage dynamically modulates the local dielectric environment, enabling sub-femtomolar (44.63 attomolar, aM) nucleic acid detection without external amplification. Clinical validation using lung cancer samples shows high concordance with quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), demonstrating real-time, label-free detection of microRNA (miRNA). This hybrid platform combines moiré-engineered optoelectronics with programmable bio-nanoarrays, offering a scalable solution for precision diagnostics with ultralow detection limits and rapid response times.
- Moiré superlattices, arising from rotational or translational mismatches between stacked periodic structures [1–3], have revolutionized the manipulation of electronic properties in two-dimensional (2D) materials. These tunable quantum platforms enable exotic phenomena, including superconductivity [4–6], Mott insulators [7] and quantum Hall effects [8,9]. Notably, twisted bilayer graphene (tBLG) superlattices, engineered through interlayer rotation, exhibit angle-dependent bandgap modulation [10,11], topological valley transport [12,13] and a moiré pattern [14,15], offering unprecedented design flexibility for optoelectronic devices. For large twist angles (>5°), tBLG preserves monolayer-like linear band dispersion near the Dirac point [16], while intersecting Dirac cones generate saddle points in the density of states (DOS), creating van Hove singularity (VHS) that enhances optical absorption and Raman resonance [17,18]. Crucially, the twist angle (θ) governs electronic coupling between atomic layers [19], enabling θ-tunable dielectric responses to external electric fields [20]. This unique angle-electric field interplay establishes tBLG as a promising candidate for moiré-enhanced optoelectronic sensors, where superlattice-specific dielectric constants can be precisely optimized through θ-selection. Although tBLG demonstrates superior optical absorption compared to monolayer graphene, realizing high photoresponsivity and selectivity in practical devices remains non-trivial. Despite theoretical predictions that VHS energy alignment with incident photon energies could enable photocurrent generation [21], experimentally achieving microampere-level photocurrents typically requires intense light irradiation (>1000 μW) [22]. To overcome this limitation, nanostructuring has been explored to enhance light-matter interactions in 2D materials [23], giving rise to non-linear phenomena, such as trion excitons [24], polaritons [25] and Rabi splitting [26]. Given tBLG's intrinsic submicrometer exciton transport length and Bohr radius, confining polaritons within tailored nanoarrays represents a viable strategy to engineer localized density of states (LDOS) near material interfaces [27]. Such LDOS enhancement can facilitate strong exciton–plasmon coupling modes [28], which are critical for optoelectronic device operation. What's more, the integration of external resonators for mode engineering may perturb structural resonances, presenting a new mechanism for innovative biosensing applications. Recently, significant advancements in biosensing have been driven by breakthroughs in materials science and sensing paradigm development [29-31]. Among optical detection techniques, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensors remain widely adopted due to their label-free, real-time detection capabilities [32]. These systems exploit refractive index changes at metal–dielectric interfaces to detect biomolecular interactions [33], which restricts their use in ultrasensitive assays. Although nanomaterial-integrated SPR sensors have achieved partial improvements through localized field enhancement, purely optical approaches fall short of the precise sub-femtomolar detection threshold required for clinical diagnostics. Here, we present a hybrid optoelectronic biosensor integrating Au nanodisks/tBLG heteroarrays with clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR)-Cas12a via DNA origami technology. Unlike conventional optical methods, this platform converts optical signals into electrical outputs through exciton–plasmon coupling in the Au nanodisks/tBLG interface, enabling ultrasensitive detection under low-light conditions. By precisely aligning the VHS absorption spectrum of tBLG with the plasmonic resonance of Au nanodisks positioned on the tBLG surface, we establish coupling modes that amplify optical transduction efficiency. This design enables amplification-free nucleic acid detection with femtomolar sensitivity, as demonstrated by the detection of single-stranded DNA at concentrations down to sub-femtomolar, exceeding traditional DNA biosensors by four orders of magnitude. Our work establishes a general framework for moiré-engineered optoelectronics, combining twist-angle-tuned dielectric properties with CRISPR-mediated specificity for multiplexed biosensing applications.

- This study establishes a paradigm for ultrasensitive biosensing through synergistic integration of twist-engineered tBLG, Au nanodisks and CRISPR-Cas12a. The angle-tuned VHSs in tBLG provide a natural pathway for low-light photocurrent generation, while DNA origami ensures precise positioning of functional elements for optimal signal transduction. The sensor's sub-femtomolar detection limit, rapid response (<1 h) and multiplexing capability surpass conventional methods by four orders of magnitude. By linking CRISPR's specificity with tBLG's unique electronic properties, we achieved label-free, real-time detection of biomarkers in complex biological matrices. This work not only advances moiré superlattice-based optoelectronics but also paves the way for next-generation clinical diagnostics with unparalleled sensitivity and specificity. This work pioneers an ultrasensitive optoelectronic biosensing platform by synergistically integrating tBLG superlattices, plasmonic nanoarrays and CRISPR-Cas12a technology. Through moiré-engineered dielectric engineering and exciton–plasmon coupling, we achieved a record-breaking photoresponsivity of 14.64 mA/W and sub-femtomolar nucleic acid detection sensitivity (44.63 aM) under low-light conditions (60 μW). This hybrid platform transcends traditional optical and electronic sensing paradigms by merging twist-angle-tunable electronic properties with programmable bio–nano interfaces. The convergence of CRISPR's molecular specificity and tBLG's moiré-enhanced light–matter interactions creates a scalable framework for multiplexed biodetection, addressing critical challenges in ultrascale diagnostics. Beyond nucleic acid sensing, this strategy holds transformative potential for detecting proteins, exosomes and other biomarkers in complex biological matrices. By pushing the boundaries of sensitivity, speed and versatility, our work charts a roadmap for next-generation precision medicine tools, where nanoscale materials and genome-editing technologies converge to unlock unprecedented diagnostic capabilities.

### From: [[../../raw/note/hanPolarTopologicalMaterials2025|hanPolarTopologicalMaterials2025]]
- ABSTRACT Polar topologies possess immense potential to revolutionize ferroelectric technology by offering nontrivial polarization configurations and a range of emergent functionalities, including unique resistive properties, negative capacitance, chirality, and ferroelectricity. Recent advancements in synthesis and characterization techniques have significantly accelerated the exploration of novel polar topological textures. This review highlights key milestones in expanding the topological family, manipulating polar topological textures through multi-field strategies, and uncovering their extraordinary functionalities, while also emphasizing future challenges and research directions. We examine the theoretical foundations and development of polar topological structures in ferroelectric materials, addressing the challenges of experimental realization and the current limitations in understanding multi-field-driven topological phase transitions, which have hindered practical implementation. Finally, this review outlines the scientific and technological prospects of polar topological structures, emphasizing their critical role in advancing materials science and device technology. We hope this review will inspire intensified research efforts that align closely with the practical applications of polar topological structures.
- Ferroelectrics, a well-established class of materials with a history spanning over a century, have seen their scope expanded to include ABO3-type perovskites, fluorite-based oxides, wurtzite structured aluminum nitride, polymers, and liquid crystals, among others. Built on the design principles, recent studies have demonstrated the promising potential for stabilizing polar topological structures in ferroelectrics. In particular, ABO3-type perovskites have drawn significant attention due to their robust ferroelectric phases and strong coupling among various order parameters, including spin, charge, orbital and lattice. This section summarizes and analyzes typical materials exhibiting polar topological structures following the design principles outlined.
- In addition to the widely studied perovskite ferroelectric heterostructures and nanostructures, other ferroelectric materials have recently emerged as promising candidates for investigating polar topological structures. These include hexagonal REMnO3 (RE, rare earths) [114], ferroelectric ceramics [115,116], organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites (OIHPs) [117], ferroelectric polymers [52,63,118], ferroelectric liquid crystal [29], and others [119]. These materials present diverse opportunities for investigating novel topologies and functionalities. For instance, Yin et al. modulated the Landau energy of Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 (BNT)-based ceramics by ions doping, leading to a transition from a non-ergodic relaxor phase to an ergodic relaxor state [115]. This results in a transformation of ferroelectric domain structures from large labyrinthine domains to more refined stripe and bubble domains, with continuous rotation of electrical dipoles (Fig. 9a).
- Benefitting from the reversible and nonvolatile switching of ferroelectric polarization driven by external fields, such as electrical, mechanical, optical, and thermal stimuli, the creation, manipulation, and dynamic motion of polar topological structures have garnered significant attention. These progresses not only provide deep physical insights into the behavior of these structures, but also unlock exotic properties that are invaluable for reconfiguring topological states and designing innovative nanoscale ferroelectric devices. Understanding the mechanisms and controlling ferroelectric polarization switching in polar topological structures is critical for integrating these polar structures into next-generation electronic devices, bringing the gap between fundamental science and practical applications.
- The most common approach to manipulating polar topological structures is through the application of electric fields, which allows for writing, detection, and erasure of these structures by modifying charge carriers, electrostatic energy and domain wall properties. Phase-field simulations have predicted the evolution of polar topological structures and phase transitions under electrical fields in ferroelectric materials. In PTO/STO superlattices, external electric fields can adjust elastic, electrostatic, and gradient energies to reduce skyrmion size, weaken inter-skyrmion interactions, and lower motion barriers [124]. Additionally, by controlling the applied electrical fields, topological transitions from polar skyrmions to different topological states have also been predicted [125,126]. Experimentally, in PTO/STO multilayers with topological polar structures, the real-time atomic resolution observation reveals the topological transformations that occur under the application of large electric fields in STEM. These include transitions from vortex to wave state, and eventually to a polarization-down state as a result of the giant electrostatic energy modulation by the electric field [127] (Fig. 10a). Similarly, in-situ STEM has also shown the lateral movement of chiral vortex cores to form close-pair structures that transform into a/c domain stripes, and eventually into a nonchiral c-domain in PTO/STO superlattices [128]. It is important to note that under strong electric fields, vortex domains in ferroelectric materials typically transition into topologically trivial domain states, with polarization aligning parallel to the applied electric field. These transformations are generally volatile, meaning that once the electric field stimulus is removed, the structure reverts to its original state, whether it is a polar vortex or a polar skyrmion [128–130]. Although the observed behavior can be ascribed to the topological protection characteristic of these structures under specific boundary conditions, the volatile nature of the switching process contradicts the stable polarization expected in ferroelectric materials. Recent studies have confirmed that nonvolatile topological transitions can be observed in larger polar topological structures under different boundary conditions imposed by an SPM probe, in contrast to those transformations driven by a TEM probe. For example, in BFO nanoislands, applying a suitable bias voltage with an SPM tip enables programmable writing of distinct topological states (vortex, center, and wedge) along with control over their conduction states [55] (Fig. 10b). In BFO thin films, radial electric field pulses have been shown to stabilize ferroelectric center states, which exhibit flux closures of antiferromagnetic spin cycloids with distinct entities at their cores depending on the field polarity [64]. Moreover, spiral and flower-like scanning-probe trajectories have been used to achieve the on-demand formation and hierarchical super-switching of intricate polar structures in strained Pb0.6Sr0.4TiO3 films [131]. These results highlight the potential for dynamic control over the complex polarization switching process by considering various electrical boundaries within the system. However, it is essential to recognize that traditional electric switching methods often come with undesirable side effects, such as leakage currents, dielectric breakdown, and charge injection, which could limit the experimental realization of topological phase transitions and hinder the practical application of polarization switching in devices. These challenges underscore the necessity for developing voltage-free strategies, such as mechanical, optical, or thermal modulation, to achieve polarization switching without compromising the integrity of the material.
- Light, owing to its robust interaction with ferroic order parameters through electromagnetic radiation (including elasto-, electro-, and magneto-optical effects), has gained prominence as both a versatile non-contact probe and a powerful tool for manipulating ferroic order parameters [136]. Specifically, the effects of light on ferroelectric polarization, which hold potential for controlling the polar topological structures, can be broadly categorized into two main types: electronic effect and thermal effect. By using sub-picosecond optical pulse excitation with photons above the bandgap energy, the electrostatic landscape can be altered to facilitate the emergence of a stable structure with long-range nanoscale polar order, consisting of polar vortices that are three-dimensionally intertwined in PTO/STO superlattices [50]. Fig. 10d depicts the formation and transition of a supercrystal phase in these superlattices, which contain ferroelectric, ferroelastic, and polar vortex sub-regions, induced by sub-picosecond optical pulses and thermal annealing. Similarly, single-shot optical pump X-ray probe measurements have captured the emergence of a persistent polar vortex supercrystal in PTO/STO heterostructures [137]. Such ultrafast light-induced phase transitions are ascribed to the perturbance of the delicate equilibrium between intrinsic built-in electrostatic and elastic frustrations by photoinduced charges. Additionally, above-bandgap light-excited charge carriers provide charge compensation for polarization-bound charges and have also been proven to contribute to lattice thermal energy, both of which are essential for stabilizing the three-dimensionally periodic nanoscale polar structures in the PTO/STO ferroelectric superlattice [138]. Moreover, twisted light, which carries a nonzero orbital angular momentum, has been found to excite and drive dynamical polar skyrmions at interfacial layers in ferroelectric PZT ultrathin films [139]. The nonzero winding number of the twisted light is transferred to the films, enabling the tuning of the intrinsic characteristics of polar skyrmions, such as their size and switching speed, by controlling the external beam parameters. Besides, mid-infrared laser light has also been demonstrated the capability to drive resonant switching of vortex tube orientation in vortex crystals in ultrathin PZT(40/60) films [140]. Therefore, optical field with varied wavelengths, powers, and other properties can trigger multiple coupling effects among electrons, phonons, and polarization, which play a significant role in manipulating polar topological structures and ferroelectrics.
- Thermal modulation, typical achieved by varying temperature, is another effective method to manipulate the degree of long-range order in polar structures, which can be exploited in optical memories [141], sensors [142], and so on. By varying the temperature, thermal phase transition and thermal expansion usually drive the change of Landu energy, symmetry anisotropy, and elastic energy. In P(VDF-TrFE), thermal modulation has been employed to control toroidal polar topologies through the pyrotoroidic transition [52]. And in PTO/STO superlattice, the real-time dynamic evolution of the polar topologies induced by thermal field has been observed based on in-situ STEM [143]. During heating, the initial polar vortices unwound the helix and transformed into polar wave and eventually into c-domain. Additionally, by designing the cooling pathways from different temperature, different polar topologies can be obtained, including polar vortex and wave. Kosterlitz-Thouless-like phase transitions have also been demonstrated in PTO/STO superlattices through temperature changes [31]. Similarly, a reversible transition from a skyrmion state to a two-dimensional tetratic lattice of merons, accompanied by a change in chirality, has been observed in (PTO16/STO16)8 membranes under varying temperature and elastic boundary conditions [144]. Besides the thermal phase transitions, external heating has also been predicted to induce the expansion of crystal lattice, thereby reducing both in-plane and out-of-plane polarization and lowering the skyrmion migration barrier without altering the skyrmion shape [124]. Although the thermal-field modulation has sparked extensive attention in multiple fields including phonon physics and infrared optics, researches are still lagged behind due to the limited characterization probe techniques.
- Polar topological structures, with their inherent topological protection and locally broken symmetry, hold promising potential for the development of multifunctional materials and devices. The switchable polarization characteristics of these structures endow them with a wide range of exceptional properties, including electric [33,40,55,65,145], optic [57,84,129], acoustic [62,146], mechanic [58,66], and magnetic functionalities [15,95], that make them highly attractive compared to their magnetic counterparts. Furthermore, the size spectrum of polar topological structures, spanning from several nanometers to micrometers, further expands their applicability across various applications.
- Among the most attractive functionalities of polar topological structures, negative capacitance has garnered extensive attention due to its rich underlying physics as well as promising applications in low-power, steep-switching and voltage-scalable electronics [31,51,59,150]. Although the nature of negative capacitance in ferroelectrics has been a subject of intense debate, intrinsic negative capacitance effects have been observed under specific conditions [151,152]. This phenomenon typically originates from the double-well profile of the ferroelectric polarization-energy landscape, which contains an unstable region characterized by negative dP/dE in isolated ferroelectric layer, often referred to as the transient negative capacitance effect. Recently, imperfect screening, driven by ferroelectric heterostructures composed of dielectric/semiconductor and ferroelectric layers, has been engineered to manipulate the free energy of the entire system, wherein the double-well energy curve of ferroelectric layer combined with the parabolic potential of the screen layer result in quasi-static (steady) negative capacitance effect. Theoretically, the negative capacitance effect is ubiquitous in multidomain ferroelectric heterostructures, where the large susceptibility of domain walls can be amplified in the vicinity of interfaces with screen layers [151]. Since the discovery of polar topological structures, the unique energy states found in local crystal structures, such as domain cores and walls, have provided favorable platforms for investigating ferroelectric negative capacitance effects. Remarkably, the steady negative capacitance state was first observed microscopically in the vortex cores of PTO/STO superlattices, as well as in the domain walls of PTO layers with suppressed polarization [59]. The authors attributed the emergence of negative permittivity at the vortex core and the higher susceptibility at the domain walls/vortex cores to their distinct polarization transition compared to the domains. Therefore, by engineering the energies of the system to adjust the size of the domain walls, the negative capacitance can be modulated. Apart from the vortex cores, local negative permittivity has also been observed at the periphery of polar skyrmions [51]. Consequently, polar topological structures offer a new opportunity for inducing steady negative capacitance in nanoscale region, owing to the exotic energetic states at the topological cores or domain walls. These breakthroughs have generated significant interest in the potential for negative capacitance to emerge in relaxor ferroelectrics and other ferroelectrics with a large density of domain walls, extending beyond polar topological structures.
- Polar topological materials, distinguished by their diverse polarization structures, have emerged as a pivotal research focus over the past decade, offering both fundamental scientific insights and promising technological applications (Fig. 14). The intricate interplay among bulk free energy, domain wall energy, elastic energy, and electrostatic energy in ferroelectrics plays a crucial role in designing these polar topological structures. This review systematically highlights significant advancements in the field and establishes key principles for developing polar topological materials. As this material family continues to expand, understanding and controlling the evolution of these structures under external stimuli remain fundamental to their applications. Furthermore, the novel functionalities arising from multi-field interactions offer exciting opportunities for next-generation applications in electronics, photonics, and magnetics. Despite rapid progress in theoretical conception and experimental realization, several challenges and unresolved questions persist, requiring further investigation to propel the field forward.

- Two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) materials consist of atomically thin layers with weak interlayer interactions. The stacking order between these layers serves as a crucial degree of freedom that governs their physical properties [1]. Altering the stacking order can give rise to a range of ferroic, strongly correlated, and topological phases with intriguing electrical, optical, and magnetic properties. Notable examples include ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic couplings in bilayer CrI3 [2–4], topological states in Weyl semimetals [5–7], valleytronics in bilayer MoS2 [8], topological transport in bilayer graphene [9,10], and superconductivity in bilayer MoTe2 [11]. Recently, sliding ferroelectricity has been experimentally observed in artificially stacked bilayer WTe2 [12], h-BN [13,14], 3R-MoS2 [15], and MoTe2 [16], where the polarization stems from the asymmetric stacking of nonpolar monolayers [17]. These findings highlight the importance of stacking order in device engineering. However, switching between different stacking orders often requires overcoming energy barriers for interlayer sliding, presenting challenges for practical manipulation [1].
- In the case of sliding ferroelectrics, for example, AB and BA stacking orders with opposite polarization can be switched via electrical fields or optical techniques [13,14,18,19]. Yet, these approaches are not always applicable to nonpolar or metallic systems [1]. Mechanical bending, however, offers a compelling alternative for manipulating the stacking orders. 2D materials, characterized by their low bending stiffness and high fracture strength, exhibit exceptional flexibility [20]. This inherent flexibility translates into strain gradients that are orders of magnitude greater than those attainable through heteroepitaxy in oxide thin films [21,22]. Thus, mechanical bending has the potential to induce strong flexoresponses in 2D materials.
- In this Letter, we propose a mechanism for controlling the stacking order in vdW layered systems through mechanically induced interlayer sliding. Leveraging the low interlayer sliding barriers in vdW materials [17,23], we demonstrate that bending deformations inevitably drive interlayer sliding, consequently altering the stacking order and associated properties. Using molecular dynamics simulation with machine-learning potentials, we investigate the effects of mechanical bending on the structural properties, energy landscapes, and electric polarizations of sliding ferroelectric bilayer h-BN and 3R-MoS2, and nonferroelectric bilayer graphene. We find mechanical bending generates irreversible kinks without significant in-plane distortion, with the kinks originating from the interplay between bending and interlayer vdW energies. These kinks correspond to domain walls that reverse out-of-plane polarization. As a result, bending deformations generate various intermediate stacking configurations, enabling the manipulation of ferroelectric domain structures. This Letter introduces an exciting mechanical bending approach for dynamically controlling stacking order and the associated optical, topological, electronic, and magnetic properties in 2D materials.
- To further investigate the bending effect, we employ a rippling deformation, a common approach in atomistic simulations. Experimentally, the rippling deformation of 2D materials can be achieved through the combination of substrate strain engineering and the all-dry transfer technique [43]. In atomic models, a sinusoidal shape was introduced to the bilayer using periodic boundary conditions. Subsequent full atomic relaxation reveals a geometric transition from the initial arclike geometry to a kinked configuration (Fig. S2 [25]). The kinks exhibit high thermodynamic stability, persisting even at high temperature of 1100 K (Fig. S11 [25]). This robust stability of kink structure results from the large in-plane stiffness of the monolayers, which confirms a recent theoretical prediction of high transition temperature for sliding ferroelectric vdW bilayers [24].
- Experimentally, bending-mediated interlayer sliding can be achieved through nanoindentation techniques applied to suspended bilayer ribbons while simultaneously performing electrical measurements [48–50]. Applying a load at the center of the suspended bilayer ribbon with a wedge-shaped tip induces bending deformation, which can be visualized using scanning electron microscopy. The persistence of kinks after unloading provides direct evidence of interlayer sliding and ferroelectric switching. Corresponding changes in stacking-related physical properties, such as polarization, can also be detected. For comparison, in the absence of interlayer sliding and ferroelectric switching, the bent monolayer ribbon would elastically return to its initial flat state upon unloading.

### From: [[../../raw/note/neumayerCompetingPolarPhases2025|neumayerCompetingPolarPhases2025]]
- Polar materials, i.e., materials that possess a spontaneous or induced electric polarization, have high relevance for condensed matter physics and materials science and underpin a wide range of current and future potential novel smart technologies. Ferroelectric (FE) materials possess spontaneous polarization that can be oriented along external electric fields. Their piezoelectric property can convert electric field to mechanical strain and vice versa, and the pyroelectricity in FE materials links thermal energy and mechanical strain.1,2 Because of the special conversion ability between different physical fields and the switchable polarization, FE materials are used as sensors, generators, resistors, and for energy storage in our daily life for medical, energy, artificial intelligence applications, etc.1,3–5 The field of microelectronics is one example where polar materials are of high importance and used in tunnel junctions, electrocalorics, and fieldeffect transistors. The increasing demand for device miniaturization and integration is challenged by Moore’s Law,6 which had tremendous effects on science and technology as well as society due to the physical limits on transistors, electrical interconnects, and memory elements at the nanoscale. To meet existing requirements, efforts have been put into modifying manufacturing process and equipment structures7,8 and, most importantly, optimizing the materials used for resistors and other electronic components.9,10 Transformational instead of incremental discoveries are needed to address and overcome current limitations. One of the fundamental difficulties associated with scaling down of ferroelectrics is the depolarization effects, which would argue for suppressed and ultimately zero polarization as the film thickness is decreased beyond a certain critical thickness in the absence of screening. This critical thickness limit is significantly affected by interface states and surface reconstructions.11,12 In addition, traditional complex metal oxides ferroelectrics are not well-suited for integration into chip board because of their incompatibility with complementary metaloxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology.13,14 To overcome scaling issues and integration problems, recent work has been focused on materials like HfO2, which shows unusual scaling behavior, where FE properties disappear above a certain thickness.15,16 In parallel, 2D FE materials have emerged as a promising material class to overcome scaling issues due to their ultra-thin nature. In addition, they have good compatibility with CMOS technology due to the pristine van der Waals (vdWs) interface and the absence of dangling bonds, which facilitates stacking with other materials via vdW interactions and, therefore, enables high-density integration of devices.17 This allows 2D ferroelectrics to be integrated into various functional devices. Although it has a short history compared to the conventional FE materials, a number of FE devices made of 2D ferroelectrics have been fabricated.
- Although van der Waals layered crystals such as CIPS crystals have been studied for 30 years,49,71 novel phenomena like the existence of in-layer and in-gap FE phases, specific interactions between ionic and polar properties, and an AFE surface phase have only been discovered recently. With the advent of big data and artificial intelligence technologies, these characteristics are particularly interesting. Increasing the number of switchable polarization states enables beyond binary data storage where more information can be stored in the same physical space. Moreover, novel pathways to change the polarization magnitude and direction arise from polar–ionic coupling and can manifest as neuromorphic behavior for energy-efficient microelectronic components. In this section, we discuss our work on switching between different FE phases as well as the reversible transitions between AFE and FE states in CIPS and CIPSe, which are all energetically close.
- Antiferroelectricity is underpinned by alternating dipole moments that cancel out to a net zero polarization in the absence of electric fields. If electric fields are applied, the dipoles align in the same direction, and the material becomes polar. AFEs are used for highdensity energy storage due to their ability to store and release large amounts of energy during field-induced transitions between polar and non-polar states. Moreover, the existence of an additional, non-polar phase facilitates beyond-binary memory applications. While the lowest energy state in CIPS is the FE phase, AFE phase stabilization has been reported in the few-layer regime.68,79 Moreover, DFT calculations revealed that interfaces with graphene and different metals (Ni, Cu, Au, and Ag) can significantly change at what thickness the AFE state is favored over the FE phase.60,80 In all cases, the interface leads to a stabilization of the FE state at fewer layers than in the freestanding case. Electrodes made of Au and Ag resulted in stabilization of the FE phase at the bilayer limit due to interfacial alignment and strain. Notably, interaction between the electrode and CIPS can also stabilize the in-gap FE phase if the material is above the critical thickness for the FE phase to form. In addition, contact doping can change the electronic structure in CIPS bilayers from insulating when interfaced with graphene to non-zero projected density of states in the bandgap when gold is used as electrode material.80 Moreover, layers that are depleted of Cu due to electric field activated Cu migration can become metallic.78 The high impact of the electrode material on CIPS properties and functionality necessitates careful selection of interfacing materials. However, interfaces are solely a static tuning knob for polar properties to facilitate desirable initial conditions and do not provide a way to interchangeably tune between FE and AFE phases.
- In some instances, the coexistence of AFE and FE phases can be observed in CIPS. Neumayer et al. discovered an AFE phase in CIPS, which was derived from a zero PFM signal due to the absence of a net polarization and, therefore, no piezoelectric material response.67 The AFE phase was localized and embedded in the FE phase. Tomographic PFM,81,82 where material is successively scraped away by the PFM probe through high contact forces, revealed that the AFE phase only appears on the surface of the sample down to a depth of 165 nm. Underneath the AFE phase was always an upward oriented domain of the FE phase. In addition to the spontaneously formed AFE surface phase, AC and DC voltages could be used to convert areas of the FE phase into the AFE phase. After applying sequences of unipolar positive, triangular DC voltages in a grid, additional non-polar areas appeared at grid locations. Piezoelectric hysteresis loops were measured at the same locations using a bipolar waveform and featured clear steps in the field-dependent piezoelectric material response at 0 pm/V, indicative of the AFE phase [Fig. 5(a)]. This transitional state is similar to pinched loops characteristics for AFE materials measured on macroscopic length scales, where at low voltages, the polarization is zero, and at higher voltages, positive or negative due to an AFE-FE phase transition. As shown in Figs. 2 and 3, the Cu ordering is distinctively different in the FE and AFE phase with more Cu disorder in the AFE phase. Due to the high Cu-ion conductivity, Cu disorder can be induced with AC voltages. After scanning an area of CIPS with 5 Vac, positively oriented þLP domains transform into the AFE phase with no PFM response [Fig. 5(b)]. The newly generated zero-response areas persisted up to a depth of 6 nm. DFT calculations confirmed that the AFE phase is energetically favored on the surface of þLP domains compared to LP domains, consistent with our experimental observations. Similar to CIPS, the coexistence of the FE and AFE phase has also been reported in CIPSe at 200 K [Fig. 5(c)].69 As for CIPS, the FE and AFE phase could be converted into each other through the application of local electric fields. The structure of CIPSe is comparable to its sulfur counterpart. However, material properties such as polarization, Curie temperature, and phase transitions are vastly different from CIPS. In turn, the FE-AFE boundary also appeared to have different properties. Most notably, a high piezoelectric material response emerged at the boundary between the two phases. In contrast, no such piezoelectric enhancement is observed at the boundaries separating the FE and AFE phases in CIPS or between the FE CIPS and the dielectric In4/3P2S6 (IPS) phase. Supported by DFT calculations and optical spectroscopy, the authors confirmed that the high-response domain walls separate areas of AFE and FE phases, which are energetically very close. The polar boundary between AFE and FE phases can be modified through electric fields, and a growth of the FE phase was observed under certain biases, constituting a novel functional element for domain-wall electronics.
- The presence of multiple polar phases with dissimilar properties in 2D layered materials with a vdW gap opens up exciting possibilities to tune material functionality and explore new device concepts. As described earlier, multiple FE phases as well as AFE phases are energetically within only a few tens of meV/f.u. As a result, they can compete, and each phase can be stabilized within the mechanical stress, electric field, and temperature parameter space potentially allowing for reversible control of polar properties and overcoming instabilities. Moreover, the small energy difference can be leveraged for low-energy devices. However, most work predicting the presence of multiple energetically close phases is theoretical, and very little experimental evidence of the more uncommon in-gap FE and AFE phases is reported. So far, only indirect evidence for the presence of these phases has been presented, mostly through PFM, which showed predicted or expected PFM values. While this is a convincing step toward evidence for the variety of polar phases in TMTPs, a direct structural verification is needed to provide indisputable proof for the existence of all polar phases. This task is challenged through the low volume content as they have been found to stabilize only locally in small volumes (in-gap FE phase) or in ultrathin samples and on surfaces (AFE phase). Therefore, macroscopic structural investigations, such as done through XRD, neutron scattering, and other approaches, would require stabilizing individual phases in larger volumes. This could be achieved through the application of mechanical pressure since the different phases of interest are predicted to have different equilibrium c-lattice parameters. Pressuredependent Raman spectroscopy has been performed on CIPS, which indicated a pressure-modified shape for the copper potential and provided evidence for the coexistence of two phases at 4.0 GPa.38 Other pressure-dependent studies revealed a pressure-related shift of ferrielectric phase transition temperature, which is opposite in CIPS and CIPSe. Theoretical work using a Landau-Devonshire approach explored the pressure-temperature phase diagram of CIPS, however, without the presence of in-gap FE or AFE phases.83 If the phases of interest cannot be stabilized in large volumes and, indeed, only occur in smaller volumes of the material, techniques like Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) can be used to directly image atomic positions if the area of interest can be isolated. However, 2D vdW materials are typically very beam-sensitive, which makes STEM studies difficult. Most STEM studies that show atomic resolution show images along the h001i axis and do not provide cross sectional information as needed to identify the Cu position with respect to the polar axis.84–86 One of the best cross-sectional image has been obtained for CCPS, where the position of Cu could be clearly identified.87 In the future, recent developments in STEM characterization and STEM sample preparation approaches such as low electron dosage imaging as well as cryo-TEM and cryo-FIB will help overcome atomic imaging challenges in the future88–90 and will provide more structural information for 2D vdW materials. Once structural information on atomic levels is available, it will be possible to provide structural proof of the existence of all polar phases. In addition, it will be possible to start studying the role of defects. So far, defects like dislocations and kinks have been observed in CIPS with TEM, which result in heterogeneous strains throughout the sample (Fig. 6).91 Atomically resolved images around defects can help understand how local phase stabilization can occur. It will also help identify local variation in chemical composition and its effect on phase stability. Defects of interest include Cu defects as Cu-ions can move across vdW gaps and re-distribute over large distances, leaving behind Cu vacancies and leading to excess Cu in different areas. It has been shown theoretically that the presence of Cu vacancies or excess Cu has direct effects on the activation barrier for Cu to cross a vdW gap under the influence of an electric field.75 In addition, DFT calculations have also shown that if a CIPS layer is depleted of half of its Cu atoms, it becomes centrosymmetric with Cu being in the middle of the P2S6 layer, therefore, losing its polar properties.50 In the case of a fully Cu-depleted CIPS layer, the electronic properties change to a metallic character.78 Therefore, the control over ion conduction and Cu/Cu vacancy distribution will play an important role in tuning the materials functionality. Another defect of potential interest in the future is substituted oxygen. While CIPS does not contain any oxygen, the application of highly localized fields under atmospheric conditions could lead to an incorporation of oxygen into the CIPS structure. This phenomenon is well known for oxides materials and might also play a role in vdW materials.92–94 Here, it is interesting to explore how the synthesis process for vdW materials can be modified to create specific structural and chemical defects in the material. Chemistry will be an interesting parameter space as well to explore in the future. TMTPs occupy a rich chemical space, where solid solutions are possible. For example, CuInP2(S,Se)6 forms solid solutions and has been studied with respect to their dielectric and electric properties. It was found that at low selenium content, the solid solutions undergo a transition to a phase with shortrange polar order, while at higher selenium content, FE ordering was suppressed.95 Furthermore, broadband dielectric spectroscopy has been used to map out the entire phase diagram, which found multiple phases including FE, relaxor, and dipolar glass phases.96 In terms of electrical conductivity, it was reported that the selenium- and sulfur-rich compositions show DC conductivity anomalies, which is not evident in the polar glass phase.97 In the future, it would be interesting to study and control the local competition of FE, PE, and glass phases with the goal to create phase coexistence where new phenomena can occur. Another aspect of chemical tunability is provided by offstoichiometry. In CIPS, it has been found that a Cu/In ratio of smaller than 1 leads to the formation of two phases: the FE CIPS phase and the dielectric IPS phase.98 The latter has a smaller c-lattice parameter and can be used to strain the CIPS phase as it was confirmed with XRD.99 This could provide a pathway to use strain as parameter to stabilize phases other than the FE phase. Since the c-lattice parameter of IPS is smaller than CIPS, this could be applied to stabilize the in-gap FE phase. In addition, the co-existence of CIPS and IPS allows for the design of in-plane heterostructures. Heterostructures of FE and paraelectric materials have been reported to result in enhanced FE and optical properties,100 vortices,101 and negative capacitance.102 Introducing dielectric spacer layers can affect the global ground state of ferroelectrics,103 including emergence of bubble-type domains with high electromechanical response.104 Overall, vdW layered FE and AFE materials enable a rich phenomenology for the next generation of multi-functional materials. While often just theoretically predicted or only demonstrated on local scales, recent work outlines the exciting opportunities these materials bring. However, the utilization of the multitude of polar behaviors and their interconversion under external stimulus in devices will necessitate to engineer desired polar phases and behavior on macroscopic length scales. Undoubtedly, progress in synthesis methods as well as characterization approaches, especially on the atomic scale, will lead to the fundamental knowledge needed for this task.

- The emergence of two-dimensional (2D) mixed transition metal MXenes has notably broadened the scope of 2D layered materials towards a wide variety of applications. Here, we report for the first time the induced ferroelectricity in free-standing Mo2Ti2C3Tx MXene film after undergoing simple heat treatment and its utilization as an active functional layer for non-volatile resistive random-access memory. The ferroelectric response, oxide phase formation and memory storage kinetics of the free-standing heated Mo2Ti2C3Tx film outperformed the pristine non-heated Mo2Ti2C3Tx. Ferroelectric behavior was observed solely in heated Mo2Ti2C3Tx owing to the formation of TiO2 with typical butterfly dielectric constant loop. Specifically, by utilizing the adaptive property of graphene oxide to reduce into conductive graphene sheets amid laser scribing, a tri-layer memristor structure Laser reduced graphene/heated Mo2Ti2C3Tx/Laser reduced graphene was fabricated and analyzed against the Laser reduced graphene/non-heated Mo2Ti2C3Tx/Laser reduced graphene device scheme. The former tri-layer free-standing scheme exhibited controlled conduction filament formation showcasing excellent resistive switching, sufficient memory window of Roff/Ron = 102 and excellent cycle-to-cycle endurance of up to 103 cycles. This finding reveals the remarkable potential of double transition MXenes to be used as functional middle layer, which was not yet explored in resistive RAM memory storage elements, hence opening the door for another domain of applications i.e. neuromorphic computing and ferroelectronics based on non-volatile memory.

The two-terminal resistive RAM devices (RRAM) work by switching the resistance states, hence storing the memory information when the voltage stress is turned off while an ultralow operating voltage is provided to the two electrode ends of the device. RRAM devices outclass other memory devices from the same non-volatile category such as Ferroelectric RAM (FeRAM), Magneto-resistive RAM (MRAM) and Phase-change RAM (PRAM) because of its unique combination of easy and cost-effective fabrication, high scalability, excellent endurance and fast switching speed, the other NVM being lacking in one property or the other [8].

- The wet chemical etching process was utilized for the synthesis of Mo2Ti2C3Tx from its MAX phase. The MAX phase was pre-washed with HCl to remove the impurities and was then subjected to HF etching. The solution (1g MAX + 10 ml HF) is allowed to homogeneously stir for 96 h at 500 rpm. Centrifugation with DI water was further done to remove acidic impurities from etched powder and maintain the pH &gt; 6 to obtain exfoliated MXene powder. The complete synthesis protocol has already been reported in Ref. [30]. The etched Mo2Ti2C3Tx powder was delaminated with the organic base 25 % TMAOH to obtain free-standing flexible film of Mo2Ti2C3Tx. The etched powder was slowly added to the 6 ml TMAOH +34 ml DI water dispersion solution, dried at 65 ◦ C @ 650 rpm for 5 h, washed and then centrifuged at 2380 rcf for an hour to separate few layers delaminated Mo2Ti2C3Tx. The free-standing MXene film was further heated at 400 ◦ C in a vacuum oven to induce ferroelectric and oxide phases that was used as an active layer in memristor structure. Drop-wise spin coating technique was used to acquire GO free-standing film after making a homogenous solution of GO powder in deionized water. The spinning was performed at 700 rpm until the solution was dried and could be lifted off from the glass substrate. This GO free standing film was passed through the laser scriber, operating at engraving speed of 900 mm/min to remove the oxygen contained species from GO and reduce it to conductive LSG.

Since all possible bilayer stackings have been discussed previously, we treat the trilayer stacking as a combination of two bilayer stackings (L1-L2 and L2-L3), which share the sandwiched L2 layer, as shown in Fig. 3(a), and perform an exhaustive search of all possible stacking configurations. The BAB, AAA, CAC, DAD, and EAF stackings are centrosymmetric, with the BAB stacking having the lowest energy, where each adjacent layer follows the AB configuration. The other stacking configurations can break inversion symmetry, unlike in trilayer graphene [31]. Since all adjacent interlayer configurations in trilayer graphene are either AB stacking or AA stacking, only three distinct stacking arrangements are possible: simple hexagonal stacking (AAA) with inversion symmetry, Bernal stacking (ABA) with mirror symmetry, and rhombohedral stacking (ABC) with inversion symmetry, none of which exhibit vertical polarization. Only when the number of layers increases does out-of-plane polarization become possible, as in the case of tetralayer graphene. In contrast, due to the more diverse stacking configurations between adjacent layers, only three layers are needed to generate out-of-plane polarization in BP. Among the polarized trilayer BP configurations, the EAB stacking is the most energetically favorable, with adjacent layers stacked in AB and AE configurations. According to our estimation using the Berry phase method, the EAB stacking with space group Pm yields an out-of-plane polarization of 0.06 pC/m and a much higher in-plane polarization of 1.32 pC/m. In addition, the energy difference between the EAB configuration and the ground-state BAB configuration is calculated to be only 40 meV, suggesting that the EAB stacking may be feasible for experimental realization.

In the case of tetralayer BP, the stacking configurations become significantly more complex. Figure 5(d) shows the kinetic pathways for transformations between neighboring out-of-plane polarization states in tetralayer BP, calculated using the CI-NEB method. The polarization switching from the maximum positive state (AEE’E) to the maximum negative state (ABAE) occurs through successive interlayer sliding processes. The system initially transitions from AEE’E through intermediate configurations AEDC, AFAB, and AFDF, during which the polarization gradually decreases until reaching the centrosymmetric AEDF state. The system then evolves toward maximum negative polarization via AEAF, ABE’B, and ABE’C states, with the polarization gradually increasing and ultimately achieving the ABAE configuration. The energy barriers associated with these transitions vary between 15 and 94 meV, with detailed atomic displacements for each sliding step shown in Fig. S2 [59]. The switching process from the maximum negative state (ABAE) to the maximum positive state (AEE’E) follows a reverse pathway with identical energy barriers. Similarly, Fig. S3 [59] demonstrates the kinetic pathways between neighboring in-plane polarization states, with barriers ranging from 15 to 96 meV. These results indicate that under appropriate electric fields, reversible switching between multiple polarization states is possible, extending beyond conventional bistable behavior and suggesting promising applications in memristive-switching devices [60,61]. Although experiments have confirmed that an out-of-plane electric field can effectively control the lateral sliding of layered materials through its coupling with out-of-plane polarization [21,23], there is still a long way to go for practical applications. - Typical early theoretical calculations have predicted ferroelasticity-based multiferroics, such as the coexistence of ferroelasticity and ferromagnetism in p-doped α-SnO monolayer and in-plane ferroelectricity and ferroelasticity in binary black phosphorus analogues (such as GeS, GeSe, SnS, and SnSe) interlocked through the unique phosphorene-isomorphic puckering square lattice (Figure 3).17−19 Subsequent studies on transition metal halides have confirmed multiferroicity and demonstrated electric tuning of magnetic moments.20−26 These findings have spurred the identification of various intrinsic and engineered 2D multiferroics, such as metal dichalcogenides,27−31 MXene,32 heterostructure,33−37 multielement compounds38−45 and doped/sliding materials12−14 (Figure 3). The preliminary progress significantly expands the range of materials with potential multiferroicity, indicating an opportunity for 2D multiferroics to complement and build upon 3D multiferroics. Motivated by these developments and inspired by them, we review 2D multiferroics to stay current with the latest progress, with a focus on artificially engineered systems. Subsequently, we also outline the challenges at the current state and offer perspectives on 2D multiferroics for unlocking future opportunities and applications in this burgeoning field.
- With the ongoing trend toward device miniaturization, significant efforts have been directed toward developing 2D multiferroic materials. In conventional magnetic perovskites like BiFeO3, lone pairs (two outer 6s electrons) in Bi3+ ions play a crucial role in inducing ferroelectric order. In contrast, 2D materials often exhibit ferroelectricity through alternative mechanisms, such as charge ordering, which is attributed to the contained transition metals with different valences or the distinct stacking configuration.47,48 Encouraged by these features, numerous strategies have been developed to artificially realize multiple ferroic orders in ultrathin 2D materials. Therefore, this section will introduce various innovative design methodologies for engineering 2D multiferroics. In addition, the specific properties of representative and emerging 2D multiferroics, including the year of discovery, thickness, Curie or Néel transition temperatures (TC or TN), spontaneous polarization value, design principles, and experimental or theoretical studies, are summarized and comprehensively compared in Table 1.
- Traditional ferroelectric perovskite thin films are usually insulators with large remanent polarization; however, they suffer from depolarization when reduced in thickness to the few-unit-cell scale. Over the past decade, significant advancements have been made in the development of 2D ferroelectrics with ultrathin thickness and high TC for memory applications.65 To further enhance multifunctionality, increasing efforts have been devoted to introducing the magnetic order into 2D anti/ferroelectrics, aiming to realize multiferroicity within a single material. The family of 2D multiferroics has since been expanded to include both ferroelectric/ferromagnetic and antiferroelectric/antiferromagnetic couplings, offering diverse combinations of ferroic orders. A notable example is the vdW multiferroic CuCrP2S6 (CCPS), which crystallizes in a monoclinic structure of low symmetry (Figure 1c).66 In the bulk phase, the Cu1+ occupies the off-center positions within S6 octahedra, giving rise to the out-of-plane electric dipole. As the incorporation of magnetic Cr3+ into the vdW layers, the CCPS exhibits interlayer antiferromagnetic ordering with a TN of approximately 30 K and intralayer stripe antiferroelectric ordering with a TC of approximately 145 K.43 Remarkably, at the 2D limit, the metastable antiferroelectric state can be switched into a stable ferroelectric state via the external voltage bias.38,44 Although the multiferroicity of CCPS originated from different cations, the unconventional magnetoelectrical coupling emerges through electric-field-driven rearrangement of Cu1+ positions within CrS6−P2S6 cages. This process stabilizes the ferroelectric state, coexisting with magnetic order. Another compelling case is the monolayer CuCrSe2, which comprises an intercalated Cu layer sandwiched between two CrSe2 layers (Figure 4c).45 The distinct vertical displacement between Cu and Se atoms induces significant out-of-plane electrical polarization. As a result, the displacement leads to shifts of the Cr 3d orbitals, thereby giving rise to ferromagnetic orders with a TC of approximately 120 K. These findings highlight the high potential of orbital engineering and structural asymmetry to achieve multiferroicity in 2D vdW systems.
- Furthermore, doping strategies have been employed to induce multiferroic behavior in 2D ferroelectric semiconductors. Moreover, it has been predicted that intentionally doping magnetic elements in specific regions of 2D ferroelectric semiconductors could lead to the coexistence of ferroelectric and ferromagnetic orders akin to diluted magnetic semiconductors. One of the typical examples is Fe-doped α-In2Se3 (Fe0.16In1.84Se3), which induces magnetic orders in 2D α-In2Se3 ferroelectric semiconductors with R-3m or P63/mmc symmetry groups (Figure 4d).12 The intrinsic ferroelectricity of Fe0.16In1.84Se3 is also enhanced by magnetic doping, providing a pathway for future exploration of 2D multiferroics with tunable ferromagnetic properties.
- Ferroelastic order typically involves the spontaneous relaxation of atoms, generating lattice strain along different directional orientations. This phenomenon can occur in lattices with centrosymmetric point groups, such as in phosphorene, and is not necessarily related to ferroelectricity. However, in certain low-symmetry 2D materials, ferroelastic and ferroelectric orders can coexist due to structural anisotropy and significant atomic displacements.18,19 For instance, groupIV monochalcogenides (e.g., SnS, SnSe, GaS, GaSe) with puckered structures have been predicted to exhibit prominent atomic displacements, which are driven by significant differences in electronegativity, leading to large spontaneous electrical polarization.19 Experimentally, ferroelectric order can be induced by modulating the interlayer stacking configuration in 2D SnSe.31 AA-stacked SnSe nanosheets exhibit sliding-induced in-plane and out-of-plane polarization, along with two types of ferroelastic domains (Figure 4e). Conversely, AB-stacked configurations, which are centrosymmetric, do not display ferroelectricity. Engineering the structural phase transition in 2D materials is also an intriguing approach to induce structural distortion or alter the stacking sequence, thereby enabling ferroelectric and ferroelastic order in a single-phase material. For example, in 2D ReS2 and ReSe2, two ferroelastic domains with two equivalent orientations are generated through the transition from the T phase (P-3m1) to the "T" phase (P-1) (Figure 4f).30 The domains can be switched by applying uniaxial strain via polymer encapsulation. The observed photovoltaic effect confirms the in-plane ferroelectricity, which can be further reoriented through ferroelastic switching. Ferroelastic domains can influence the ferroelectric order through strain engineering, thereby enhancing the coupling between the multiferroic orders and providing a promising pathway for developing innovative 2D multiferroics.
- Although intriguing magnetoelectric coupling behaviors have been observed in 3D or thin film heterostructures, the development of 2D vdW heterostructure multiferroics remains limited.74 Integrating individual ferroelectric and ferromagnetic components into a heterostructure by sequential deposition or mechanical stacking may be a more straightforward fabrication strategy and more likely to be applied in magnetoelectric devices capable of "electric writing" and "magnetic reading". The key advantage of 2D multiferroics lies in their atomically thin thickness and atomically flat surfaces, which enable clean interfacial contact and compelling device architectures through stacking. For example, the heterostructure multiferroics composed of 2D vdW ferromagnetic Cr2Ge2Te6 and the ferroelectric thin film P(VDF-TrFE) have been achieved by mechanical stacking (Figure 4g).41 The polarization switching in the ferroelectric thin film enables the reversible control of coercive field and spin polarization of the Cr2Ge2Te6 ferromagnetic layer, suggesting the potential feasibility of magnetoelectric coupling toward device applications. Regarding all-vdW layered heterostructures, the bilayer Cr2Ge2Te6/In2Se3 multiferroics were theoretically predicted to exhibit strong interlayer magnetoelectric coupling effects. The reversal of the electric polarization of the In2Se3 monolayer can alter the magnetic anisotropy of the Cr2Ge2Te6 monolayer between out-of-plane and in-plane orientations.33 A recent demonstration of an all-vdW layered heterostructure has been fabricated by exfoliation and vertically stacking the ferromagnetic Fe2.64GeTe2 and ferroelectric α-In2Se3 nanoflakes.37 In this heterostructure, the magneto-optic microscopy reveals that the coercive field of the Fe2.64GeTe2 layer appears to have a prominent decrease upon the application of alternating voltages. Nevertheless, the voltage-dependent change in magnetic property is nonreversible and noncyclic, which is ascribed to the strain variation at the interface of the heterostructure, originating from the piezoelectric In2Se3 layer, rather than a direct and switchable magnetoelectric coupling. Such a configuration has also been applied to other magnetic 2D materials to construct all-van der Waals (vdW) layered multiferroics, such as LaCl/α-In2Se3,35 Fe3GeTe2/α-In2Se3,75 and CrI3/α-In2Se3.76 However, most of these heterostructures have yet to demonstrate direct and switchable magnetoelectric coupling in experiments, highlighting a broader challenge even within conventional multiferroic fabrication strategies. The weak or absent interfacial coupling between assembled ferroelectric and ferromagnetic layers may be attributed to factors such as screening effects, unfavorable band alignment, and interfacial strain relaxation.39,76 These findings highlight the promise of 2D heterostructure multiferroics for the development of low-power, nonvolatile, and highly tunable memory and logic devices. Moving forward, systematic interface engineering, precise stacking control, and operando characterization techniques are crucial for uncovering the potential fundamental mutual coupling mechanisms and their applications.
- The above advancements confirm the feasibility of designing 2D van der Waals multiferroics, with several successful cases demonstrated. However, future development still faces significant challenges in the development of 2D multiferroics, particularly in material preparation, performance improvement, and potential applications.
- Intrinsic 2D multiferroics remain scarce due to the mutual exclusivity of multiple ferroic orders in many materials, whereas the structural flexibility of a 2D configuration can increase the likelihood of achieving multiferroicity. Notably, 2D magnetic materials, predominantly transition metal-based compounds, are more prevalent than their ferroelectric or ferroelastic counterparts. Recent reports have shown that breaking centrosymmetry is easier in 2D systems. Some centrosymmetric 2D materials exhibit notable ferroelectricity, which is often enhanced by structural distortions or defects. In particular, light elements, Such as S or Se, can depict displacements and result in obvious ferroelectricity, differing from the metal atom displacements typically observed in 3D ferroelectrics. This deviation from traditional 3D ferroelectric behavior offers avenues for developing 2D multiferroics. As mentioned above, focusing on transition metal compounds and incorporating structural processing methods presents a feasible approach for preparing 2D multiferroics. Comparatively, ferroelastic-related 2D multiferroics are still less reported, highlighting the importance of developing materials with ultralow symmetry structures. Due to the high crystal quality and ease of availability, mechanical exfoliation remains a preferred method for preparing intrinsic multiferroics, facilitating fundamental property investigations and material screening. However, chemical vapor deposition or thin-film deposition may open up more possibilities in engineering, as substrate-induced strain, defects, or distortions are more likely to occur.
- Maintaining considerable multiferroicity and mutual coupling at room temperature is the key section and challenge for practical applications. In 2D systems, thermal fluctuations are more pronounced, resulting in a low thermal conductivity (TC) of most 2D materials below room temperature. However, some 2D materials exhibit reliable ferroelectric orders at room temperature, such as In2Se3,77 CrTe2,78 and ReS2,79 providing promising frameworks for further development. To achieve stable room-temperature 2D multiferroics, it is essential to establish a stable crystal structure that prevents symmetry restoration and supports long-range order driven by strong exchange interactions. Metal atom doping, defects, and intercalation (e.g., transition metals like Co, Mn, Cr, Ni, or rare-earth elements) can enhance unpaired spin density and crystal asymmetry. Another approach is to directly combine ferromagnetic and ferroelectric layers with the TC above RT into heterostructures, which can induce interfacial exchange bias, charge transfer, and orbital reconstruction. However, the interface quality is crucial in strengthening multiferroic coupling. Ion gating or electrostatic doping can also adjust electron density and alter ferroic orders, providing another avenue for tuning multiferroic behavior. With the increase in reports on 2D intrinsic multiferroics, highthroughput computing and machine learning will play a crucial role in predicting and screening emerging 2D multiferroic materials. Enhancing the coupling coefficient between different ferroic orders is essential for advancing high-performance devices. An intuitive approach for improving the coupling coefficient is manufacturing 2D multiferroics with identical origins for all ferroic orders. Atom intercalation between van der Waals layers presents a feasible method for simultaneously modulating the density of states and symmetry. The quasi-layered structures also provide a ready framework for substituting intercalation atoms, such as Cu(CrS2)2, a multiferroic material where Cu can act as active replacement sites. Additionally, regulating the ligands and coordination environment of magnetic metal atoms in 2D materials also unifies the origins of ferroelectricity and magnetism, further enhancing the overall multiferroic properties. However, the suppression of the nonempty d shell on polarization needs to be addressed.
- There are no definitive reports of the device applications based on 2D multiferroics. However, we can draw insights from 3D multiferroics, in which the applications of ferroelectricferromagnetic multiferroics are more attractive because they offer promising prospects for reducing power consumption and improving storage and computing performance. By harnessing electric-field control of magnetism in atomically thin layers, spin-current logic gates that switch magnetic states via gate voltage with minimal Joule heating, 2D magnetic tunnel junctions in which a ferroelectric barrier modulates tunneling magnetoresistance with nonvolatile memory bits, memristors that exploit coupled polarization and magnetization to achieve multistate resistive switching for neuromorphic computing, may be more feasible and effective. Among them, the device architecture design is crucial for maximizing performance and functionality. With continued advances in material synthesis and performance improvement, the 2D multiferroic devices may soon be realized.

- The emergence of sliding ferroelectricity in 2D van der Waals materials has opened a new paradigm for designing nanoscale nonvolatile memories, where polarization switching is governed by interlayer sliding rather than conventional ion displacement. This perspective highlights materials, strategies, switching dynamics, and device applications of 2D sliding ferroelectric materials. Finally, the critical challenges and future research directions are outlined, aiming to guide the rational design of nextgeneration sliding ferroelectric materials.
- Ferroelectric materials have attracted enduring scientific interest due to their unique physical properties arising from spontaneously aligned electric dipoles that can be collectively switched by external electric fields1–3. This remarkable feature, combined with their nonlinear dielectric response and piezoelectric effects, has enabled widespread applications in nonvolatile memory devices, sensors, acoustic wave filters, and optoelectronic systems4,5. However, conventional bulk ferroelectric materials face significant challenges in meeting the demands of modern electronic device development. Their inherent thickness limitations, mechanical rigidity, and the requirement for relatively high switching fields present fundamental obstacles for applications requiring miniaturization, high-density integration, and mechanical flexibility. The emergence of two-dimensional (2D) materials has opened new possibilities for ultrathin ferroelectric systems. While these atomically thin materials offer several advantages including exceptional mechanical flexibility and reduced switching fields, their practical implementation faces multiple fundamental challenges. First, the stabilization of ferroelectric order in reduced dimensions is severely constrained by the critical size effect, where depolarization fields become increasingly prominent as material thickness decreases. Second, enhanced surface effects in 2D systems lead to significant interface charge trapping and screening effects. Third, substrateinduced strain and interfacial interactions can substantially modify or even suppress intrinsic ferroelectric properties. Although theoretical studies have predicted promising candidates with unconventional ferroelectric mechanisms, such as WO2Cl2 where competing soft modes induce noncollinear ferrielectricity, experimental realizations remain scarce6. On the experiment front, the above mentioned factors have resulted in a remarkably limited family of confirmed 2D ferroelectric materials, with only a few systems such as α-In2Se37,8, CuInP2S69–11, CuInP2Se612, SnTe13, NbOI214, and Bi2O2Se15, demonstrating robust ferroelectricity at 2D limit.
- Sliding ferroelectricity in 2D van der Waals (vdW) materials has provided groundbreaking solutions to these long-standing challenges. Distinct from conventional ionic-type ferroelectrics, this novel mechanism originates from interlayer charge transfer in non-centrosymmetric stacking configurations. The concept was first proposed in 2017 by Wu’s group16, they proposed a revolutionary concept through first-principles calculations that even when monolayers possess centrosymmetric structures (lacking intrinsic ferroelectricity), carefully engineered bilayer or multilayer stacking could break spatial inversion symmetry to generate robust out-of-plane (OOP) polarization. This discovery has fundamentally expanded the scope of ferroelectric materials17–20. Subsequent experimental verifications in various systems, including bilayer WTe221–23, β-InSe24, boron nitride (BN)25,26, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs, e.g., WSe2, MoSe2, WS2, MoS2)27, 1T’ReS228, γ-InSe29, ε-InSe30, and multilayer hexagonal bron nitride (hBN)31 (Fig. 1), have firmly established sliding ferroelectricity as a universal phenomenon in vdW materials. The universality of this phenomenon stems from its geometric origin—any 2D system where sliding modifies interlayer charge asymmetry can, in principle, exhibit sliding ferroelectricity. This fundamental insight has expanded the design space for 2D ferroelectrics beyond the constraints of conventional ionic displacement mechanisms and offers several unprecedented advantages: (i) it effectively circumvents the pinning effects caused by defect charges, significantly enhancing endurance and reliability32,33; (ii) the polarization state depends solely on interlayer stacking configuration, enabling novel multistate memory and computing applications; and (iii) the polarization can be modulated through diverse external stimuli including electric fields, mechanical stress, and strains, providing exceptional tunability.
