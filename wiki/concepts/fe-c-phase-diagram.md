# Fe-C Phase Diagram (铁碳相图)

The iron-carbon (Fe-C) phase diagram is the fundamental thermodynamic framework used to characterize the equilibrium states and phase transformation boundaries in steels and cast irons. It maps the stability regions of various phases—primarily $\alpha$-ferrite (BCC), $\gamma$-austenite (FCC), and cementite ($\text{Fe}_3\text{C}$, orthorhombic)—as a function of temperature and carbon concentration. In the context of industrial processing, the diagram serves as the critical reference for determining thermodynamic limits such as the $A_{e3}$ temperature, which defines the start of the austenite-to-ferrite transformation during cooling [[../papers/Zhang2002b]].

### Physical Mechanisms and Phase Stability
The stability of phases in the Fe-C system is governed by the minimization of Gibbs free energy, which is deeply rooted in the crystal structure and electronic configuration of the iron lattice. The transformation from $\gamma$ to $\alpha$ involves a structural transition from the close-packed face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice to the body-centered cubic (BCC) lattice. Because the interstitial sites in the BCC structure are significantly smaller than those in the FCC structure, the solubility of carbon is drastically reduced (from ~2.11 wt% in $\gamma$ to ~0.0218 wt% in $\alpha$ at the eutectoid temperature). This disparity necessitates the redistribution of carbon atoms, making the $\gamma \rightarrow \alpha$ phase transformation a diffusion-controlled process [[../papers/Zhang2002b]].

### Thermodynamic Driving Force and Undercooling
During continuous cooling, the system deviates from the equilibrium boundaries defined by the phase diagram. The difference between the equilibrium transformation temperature ($A_{e3}$) and the actual local temperature ($T$) defines the undercooling ($\Delta T$), which provides the chemical driving force for the nucleation and growth of new grains. According to the classical theory of phase transformations, this driving force must overcome the interfacial energy barrier to initiate the formation of ferrite nuclei, typically at high-energy sites such as austenite grain boundaries [[../papers/Zhang2002b]].

### Influence of Alloying Elements
In real-world low-carbon steels, alloying elements such as Si, Mn, and Cr modify the Fe-C phase boundaries. These effects can be modeled using the "super-element approximation," which linearizes the contribution of various solutes to the total Gibbs free energy change ($\Delta G^{\gamma \rightarrow \alpha}$). By incorporating Zener parameters that reflect the electronic and magnetic contributions of each element to phase stability, the effective $A_{e3}$ and local equilibrium concentrations ($c_{\gamma}$, $c_{\alpha}$) are shifted, directly influencing the kinetics of microstructure evolution and the final grain size [[../papers/Zhang2002b]].
